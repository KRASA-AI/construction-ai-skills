# Lien Waivers by State — Reference

> Reference for skills that draft, review, or assemble lien-waiver packages
> for construction contractors and material suppliers in the United States.
> This document is not legal advice. State statutes and case law evolve;
> verify with construction counsel for any project-specific or large-dollar matter.

---

## Purpose of This Reference

A **lien waiver** is a contractor's, subcontractor's, or supplier's signed acknowledgment that, in exchange for payment (or upon receipt of payment), it waives some or all of its mechanic's-lien or payment-bond claim rights for the labor and materials covered by that payment. The waiver is what owners and lenders rely on to release funds and close out a project free of lien exposure.

The waiver is a contract-of-adhesion-style document drafted under the shadow of state statute. The dimensions that vary state-to-state are: (a) whether a specific statutory form is required or merely permitted, (b) whether notarization is required, (c) what the four canonical waiver moments are called and when each is appropriate, (d) whether prospective waivers (waivers signed in advance of payment or in advance of work) are enforceable, (e) the statutory deadline by which a lien must otherwise be filed, and (f) special wrinkles like joint-check rules, residential-vs-commercial carve-outs, and trust-fund statutes.

This reference summarizes the common practitioner-level rules. It is intentionally brief — for a state-specific dispute, retain counsel licensed in that state and pull the current statute text.

---

## The Four Canonical Lien-Waiver Moments

Every state recognizes some version of these four waiver categories, though names vary:

1. **Conditional waiver on progress payment** — Waives lien rights for work done through a stated date, but only effective once the specific progress payment clears (usually when the check clears the bank or wire is confirmed). This is the safe waiver to sign in exchange for a progress payment that has not yet cleared.
2. **Unconditional waiver on progress payment** — Waives lien rights for work done through a stated date, immediately and irrevocably. Should be signed only **after** payment has been received and cleared. Signing this in exchange for a check that later bounces typically means lien rights are gone.
3. **Conditional waiver on final payment** — Waives all lien rights for the project, effective only once the final payment clears. Used at closeout in exchange for the final retainage release.
4. **Unconditional waiver on final payment** — Waives all lien rights for the project, immediately and irrevocably. Signed after final payment has cleared; the closeout document the owner uses to confirm a lien-free turnover.

The pattern under every state's law is the same: sign **conditional** in exchange for promised payment, sign **unconditional** only after payment has actually cleared. Skills that auto-fill waivers should default to conditional when the payment status is unknown.

## States with Mandatory Statutory Forms

In these states, a lien waiver is enforceable only if it substantially follows the form prescribed by statute. Custom or out-of-state forms can be set aside as unenforceable. Twelve states fall in this category, though sources occasionally vary on Florida (which permits but does not strictly mandate the statutory form). Verify the current statute before relying on a custom form in any of these jurisdictions.

| State | Statute (citation) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Arizona | Ariz. Rev. Stat. §§ 33-1008 | Four canonical forms; specific wording mandated |
| California | Cal. Civ. Code §§ 8132, 8134, 8136, 8138 | Four canonical forms; "Notice to Claimant" warning text required at the prescribed type size |
| Florida | Fla. Stat. § 713.20 | Statutory form is the safe form; parties cannot require non-statutory wording, though custom may be acceptable in narrow circumstances. Treat as mandatory in practice. |
| Georgia | O.C.G.A. § 44-14-366 | Specific statutory wording required; "Interim Waiver and Release Upon Payment" is the progress-payment form |
| Massachusetts | Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 254, § 32 | Statutory form for partial waivers on residential and most private work |
| Michigan | Mich. Comp. Laws § 570.1115 | Specific wording for partial and full unconditional waivers |
| Mississippi | Miss. Code §§ 85-7-433 et seq. | Statutory forms required; **notarization required** (see below) |
| Missouri | Mo. Rev. Stat. ch. 429 | Statutory waiver-form mechanics live in chapter 429; verify the current section before relying on a specific cite. Residential consent-of-owner / 10-day-notice rules are separate and reside in § 429.012 |
| Nevada | Nev. Rev. Stat. §§ 108.2457 | Four canonical forms; specific wording required |
| Texas | Tex. Prop. Code §§ 53.281–53.287 | Four canonical forms in § 53.284; notarization was repealed by HB 2237 — verify the effective-date carve-out (commonly cited as 2021 or 2022 depending on source) before relying on an unnotarized waiver from the transition period. Notarization is not required for current waivers. Joint-check rules are state-specific |
| Utah | Utah Code § 38-1a-802 | Statutory form for waivers; SCR (State Construction Registry) interacts with notice timing |
| Wyoming | Wyo. Stat. § 29-1-301 | Statutory form required; **notarization required** to be enforceable |

In all twelve states, modifying the statutory form's substantive text — even to add a customary "for good and valuable consideration" recital or a project-administrator's signature line — risks rendering the waiver void. Add-ons (project number, sub-tier identification, the joint-payee note) that do not alter the statutory text are generally safe. When in doubt, use the statutory form unaltered and put any project-specific cover or context on a separate transmittal page.

### States Requiring Notarization

- **Mississippi** — Final unconditional waiver typically requires notarization; some forms require multiple notarizations in the chain.
- **Wyoming** — All lien waivers must be notarized to be enforceable.

Texas previously required notarization; the requirement was repealed by HB 2237 with an effective-date carve-out commonly cited as January 1, 2021 or January 1, 2022 in different industry summaries. Waivers signed during the transition window should be checked against the statute as enacted at the time of signing. Current Texas waivers do not require notarization.

## States Without Mandatory Statutory Forms

The remaining states permit any reasonably worded lien waiver — typically a recital of the consideration, the work covered, the date through which lien rights are waived, and whether the waiver is conditional or unconditional. Best practice is still to use a clean four-corner waiver that mirrors the canonical moments above and that is unambiguous about whether it is conditional or unconditional. Plain English plus a clear "this waiver is effective only upon clearance of payment of $X to claimant" line resolves most enforceability disputes.

In these states, the bigger risks are:

- **Prospective waivers** (waivers signed in advance, often in the subcontract itself, attempting to waive future lien rights): unenforceable in many states by statute or case law (e.g., New York Lien Law § 34, the Illinois Mechanics Lien Act at 770 ILCS 60/1 et seq., and similar statutes elsewhere). A prospective-waiver clause buried in a subcontract may be an unenforceable trap; check the project state.
- **Joint-check rules** — Varies state by state; some states require a joint-check waiver to clearly identify the upstream and downstream payees and the consideration flowing to each.
- **Residential-vs-commercial carve-outs** — Several non-mandatory-form states still mandate specific notice or disclosure language on residential projects (e.g., owner-occupied single-family).

## State-Lien-Filing Deadlines (At a Glance)

Lien waivers extinguish lien rights for the work covered; the lien-filing deadline matters when payment is not received and a claimant must assert lien rights. Common deadline patterns:

- **California:** 90 days after completion or cessation of work; 60 days after recorded Notice of Completion (where applicable)
- **Texas:** Generally 15th of the 3rd or 4th month after the month the work was performed (commercial vs. residential differ)
- **Florida:** 90 days after final furnishing
- **New York:** 8 months on commercial; 4 months on single-family residential
- **Illinois:** Sub-tier 90-day notice (770 ILCS 60/24) and a 2-year suit window (770 ILCS 60/9); contractor's lien recording rules are separate
- **Arizona:** 60 to 120 days depending on completion-date triggers
- **Georgia:** 90 days after last work
- **Most other states:** 60–120 days after last work or substantial completion

Always verify the current statute before relying on these — deadlines are routinely amended by state legislatures, and several have notice-pre-conditions (preliminary notice, monthly notice, notice of intent to lien) that are jurisdictional.

## Preliminary-Notice Requirements (Quick Map)

Many states require a sub-tier claimant to send a preliminary notice within a defined window after first furnishing labor or materials in order to preserve later lien rights. A waiver downstream does not retroactively cure a missed preliminary notice. Examples:

- **California:** 20-day preliminary notice (Civ. Code § 8200 et seq.)
- **Arizona:** 20-day preliminary notice
- **Florida:** Notice to Owner within 45 days
- **Texas:** Monthly fund-trapping notice and/or pre-lien notice depending on tier and project type
- **Nevada, Utah, Washington:** State Construction Registry-style preliminary notice systems
- **New York:** No preliminary notice for private commercial; specific procedures for public-works claims

Skills that draft waivers should remind the user to verify preliminary-notice status before treating a waiver as the cure for a payment dispute.

## Common Drafting Pitfalls

A skill that drafts or reviews waivers should flag the following:

1. **"Through" date is wrong** — A waiver should waive rights for work through a stated date. If the date is the sub's last day on site, the waiver waives everything; if it is an interim payment date, only that portion. Default to the date through which the corresponding payment covers — usually the period-ending date on the pay application.
2. **Conditional/unconditional mismatch** — Skill should default to conditional unless the user confirms the payment has cleared.
3. **Modifying statutory text in a mandatory-form state** — Flag. Tell the user the modification may render the waiver unenforceable; recommend the unaltered statutory form plus a separate cover page for any context.
4. **Notarization missed in a notarization state** — Flag. In Mississippi and Wyoming the waiver is unenforceable without notarization. Build in a "send to notary" step before transmittal.
5. **Backcharge interaction** — When a backcharge has been deducted from a pay application, the lien-waiver should reflect the gross billed amount less the deduction; the claimant's lien rights as to the disputed deduction are preserved by an explicit reservation. Skills that handle backcharges (`skills/admin/backcharge-notice-drafter.md`) should reference this rule and coordinate with the lien-waiver.
6. **Joint-check waivers** — Where the payment is by joint check (owner-GC-sub or GC-sub-supplier), the waiver should identify the joint payees and the flow of funds, or use the state's joint-check waiver variant. Improper joint-check handling is a common source of double-payment claims.
7. **"All claims" overrides** — Some custom owner forms include "and all other claims" language that, signed unconditionally, can sweep delay, change-order, or extra-work claims into the waiver. Flag for review; default is to limit the waiver to lien and bond-claim rights only, not all damages.
8. **Public-work bond claims** — On federal Miller Act and state Little Miller Act projects, a "lien waiver" really waives the payment-bond claim, not a lien (no liens on public works). Use the state's bond-claim waiver where one exists; where none exists, use a clean four-corner bond-claim waiver mirroring the lien-waiver structure.
9. **Trust-fund states** — Some states (e.g., Texas, Maryland, New York) treat construction-contract funds as held in trust for downstream claimants. A waiver does not extinguish trust-fund claims if the upstream payor diverted funds. Note in the waiver: "This waiver does not waive trust-fund claims under [state statute]."
10. **Prospective-waiver clauses** — Flag any subcontract or PO clause attempting to waive future lien rights as a condition of award. In several states this is unenforceable; in others it is merely disfavored.

## How Skills Should Use This Reference

Skills that draft or process lien waivers (the planned `Lien Waiver Drafter` skill) should:

1. **Detect the project state from `config.yml` or required input.** The state determines whether a statutory form is required and whether notarization is required.
2. **Default to the conditional-on-progress-payment form** unless the user confirms payment has cleared, in which case switch to the unconditional form.
3. **Use the statutory form unaltered** in mandatory-form states. If the user requests a modification to the statutory text, refuse and explain why.
4. **Add a notarization step** for Mississippi and Wyoming, and a notarization-not-required note for all others.
5. **Cross-check the through-date** against the corresponding pay application's period-ending date.
6. **Reference this document** for the state-specific notes and prompt the user to verify the preliminary-notice status separately.
7. **Coordinate with any active backcharge** — a deduction-affected pay application requires a lien-waiver that reflects the gross-less-deduction billing and explicitly preserves the claimant's rights to the disputed amount.

Skills that process closeout (`skills/admin/closeout-documentation-auditor.md`) should:

1. **Confirm a final-payment lien-waiver from every direct sub** before recommending retainage release.
2. **Flag any final-payment lien-waiver that is unconditional but where payment has not yet cleared.** This is the most common closeout error.
3. **Check the state's mandatory-form posture** before accepting a non-statutory closeout waiver in a mandatory-form state.
